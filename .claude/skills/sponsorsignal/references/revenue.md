# How this makes money

Read `SKILL.md` first. This file is the detail behind "who pays".

## The ranking, most realistic first

### 1. Business subscriptions — the real business

Recruitment agencies, immigration advisers, relocation firms and HR teams.
They have budgets, they renew, and they want the thing only we hold: what
changed on the register.

What they will pay for:

- A weekly or daily feed of newly licensed and newly revoked sponsors,
  filtered to their sector or region.
- CSV or JSON export of the current register, cleaned and tagged.
- A simple API endpoint, or even just a stable file URL they can poll.
- Historical answers: "how many care sponsors were licensed in Q1", "when did
  this employer first appear", "which sponsors lost their licence last month".

Realistic pricing: £49–£199 a month. Do not undercharge here. A recruiter who
places one sponsored candidate earns thousands; £99 a month is noise to them
and they will not take a £9 tool seriously.

This is the highest-value work, but it depends on change history existing. That
is why capturing additions and removals outranks almost everything else.

### 2. Free tier for job seekers — the engine, not the revenue

Keep the search free forever. Job seekers are the traffic, the search rankings,
the word of mouth and the reason the business is defensible. They are not the
customer, and building the product around extracting money from them would be
both ineffective and unkind.

A cheap personal alert tier (£5–£9 a month for filtered alerts) can work once
the list is large, but treat it as a bonus, not the plan. Expect low conversion:
this audience is genuinely short of money.

### 3. Sponsored placement and advertising

A small, high-intent, well-defined audience is worth real money to the right
advertiser: CV services, IELTS and English test prep, international money
transfer, relocation and shipping, credit builders for new arrivals.

Rules: label paid placement clearly, never let it distort search results, never
sell the email list. One tasteful sponsor in the newsletter is worth more than
a page full of banners, and does not wreck the experience.

### 4. Referrals to immigration advisers — highest value, highest care

Immigration solicitors pay a lot per qualified lead. This is genuinely
lucrative and genuinely risky.

UK immigration advice is regulated. We must stay clearly on the side of
advertising, never advice: no case assessment, no eligibility scoring, no
"you qualify for X". Keep the existing disclaimers, link out plainly, and only
work with OISC-registered or SRA-regulated advisers. **Ask the user before
building anything in this lane.**

## Sequencing

Each of these unlocks the next. Doing them out of order wastes the work.

1. **Collect emails.** Nothing else compounds without it.
2. **Accumulate change history.** It needs calendar time, so start now.
3. **Send something worth reading, weekly.** A digest nobody opens is not an
   asset. Aim for one genuinely useful email a week.
4. **Earn traffic.** Search Console, landing pages, launch posts.
5. **Then sell.** By this point there is an audience, a dataset and evidence of
   what people actually want, and pricing stops being guesswork.

## What to measure

Without these, everything above is opinion:

- Weekly visitors, and which pages they land on.
- Email signups, and signup rate per hundred visitors.
- Newsletter open rate and click rate.
- Which searches and filters people actually use.
- How many new and removed sponsors are detected each week.

## Things that will not work

Be direct with the user about these rather than politely exploring them:

- **Paywalling the search.** The data is free and public. Someone will clone it
  in an afternoon and the goodwill is gone.
- **Ads plastered over the search results.** Destroys the experience for a few
  pounds and undermines the trust the paid product depends on.
- **Selling or renting the email list.** Ends the business.
- **Scraping employer contact details to sell.** Data protection risk, and a
  completely different, much uglier business.
- **AI-generated blog spam for SEO.** Google is actively demoting it, and it
  would contradict the entire premise of being the accurate source.
